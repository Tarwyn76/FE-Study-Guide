## 15.1 Hydrocarbon Families and Carbon Skeletons

Organic chemistry is organized around carbon bonding. The Handbook lists common families including alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic compounds.

Alkanes contain only single carbon-carbon bonds. Alkenes contain at least one double bond; alkynes contain at least one triple bond. Aromatic compounds contain delocalized ring systems such as benzene.

Unsaturation changes reactivity and hydrogen count. For simple acyclic hydrocarbons, the Handbook notes patterns such as \(C_nH_{2n}\) for one-alkene systems and \(C_nH_{2n-2}\) for one-alkyne systems.

## 15.2 Functional Groups and Nomenclature

The Handbook identifies major families such as alcohols, ethers, amines, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, and haloalkanes.

A **functional group** is the reactive structural feature used to classify an organic compound. Naming rules identify the parent carbon chain and functional group, then locate substituents.

For FE problems, the goal is often recognition rather than exhaustive IUPAC naming. Be able to associate \(OH\) with alcohol, \(COOH\) with carboxylic acid, carbonyl structures with aldehyde/ketone, and ester linkage with \(COOR\).

## 15.3 Organic Reaction Patterns

A full organic-reaction catalog is outside the shared core, but common engineering patterns include combustion, oxidation/reduction, addition across multiple bonds, substitution, hydrolysis, esterification, and polymerization.

The balanced-reaction methods from Chapter 02-12 still apply. Organic reactions conserve atoms just like inorganic reactions.

## 15.4 Cells and Biological Organization

The Handbook provides diagrams of animal, plant, and bacterial cells. The shared structural idea is a boundary membrane surrounding cellular machinery; eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles, while bacteria have a simpler internal organization.

Important structures include nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, cell membrane, cell wall where present, chloroplasts in photosynthetic plant cells, and bacterial chromosome.

Engineering biology often treats the cell as a reaction system: nutrients and electron donors enter, biomass/products and waste leave, and enzyme-controlled reactions transform matter and energy.

## 15.5 Enzymes, Proteins, and Genetic Information

Proteins perform structural, transport, signaling, and catalytic functions. **Enzymes** are biological catalysts: they alter reaction rates without changing the underlying equilibrium position.

Genetic information is stored in nucleic acids and expressed through transcription and translation. The detailed molecular machinery is route-specific, but the shared-core concept is that biological function depends on regulated information flow and protein synthesis.

Temperature, pH, inhibitors, and substrate concentration can affect enzyme activity.

## 15.6 Photosynthesis, Respiration, and Bioconversion

The Handbook gives the overall photosynthesis reaction

\[
6CO_2+6H_2O+\text{light}\rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2.
\]

It also provides representative aerobic and anaerobic biodegradation equations. In engineering, these equations support material balances and yield calculations.

Aerobic processes use oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor; anaerobic processes operate without molecular oxygen and may produce methane and carbon dioxide. Biological yields connect substrate consumption to cell or product formation.

## 15.7 Analytical Methods for Chemical and Biological Systems

The Handbook includes a table showing whether common instrumental methods provide qualitative or quantitative elemental or molecular information.

Examples include atomic absorption, atomic emission, chromatography, mass spectrometry, infrared spectroscopy, NMR, UV/visible spectrophotometry, and X-ray methods.

Choose a method based on the information required. Elemental concentration is a different analytical question from molecular identity. Separation methods are different from spectroscopic identification methods, though instruments are often coupled.
