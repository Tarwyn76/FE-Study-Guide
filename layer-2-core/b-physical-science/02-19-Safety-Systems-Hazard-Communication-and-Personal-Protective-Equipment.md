## 19.1 Safety, Hazard, Risk, and Exposure

The Handbook defines safety as protection from threats or failures and control of known threats to an acceptable level of risk.

A **hazard** is the inherent capacity to cause harm. **Risk** combines hazard with the probability or degree of exposure:

\[
Risk=Hazard\times Probability
\]

or, for many health hazards,

\[
Risk=Hazard\times Exposure.
\]

Reducing exposure can reduce risk even when the underlying hazard remains.

## 19.2 Job Safety Analysis and Hazard Identification

The Handbook describes Job Safety Analysis (JSA), also called JHA/AHA, as a task-based process that breaks a job into steps, identifies potential hazards, and documents safer methods.

Useful questions include whether a worker can be struck, caught, pinched, overexerted, or exposed to toxic gases, dusts, heat, electricity, or radiation.

A JSA is strongest when performed by people who understand the actual task and when controls are tied to specific steps rather than generic warnings.

## 19.3 Hazard Controls and the Control Hierarchy

A common guide-developed framework is the hierarchy of controls:

1. eliminate the hazard,
2. substitute a less hazardous process/material,
3. use engineering controls,
4. use administrative/work-practice controls,
5. use personal protective equipment.

Higher-level controls generally reduce dependence on perfect human behavior.

PPE is important but is usually the last line of defense rather than the first design choice.

![FIG-02-19-003: Hierarchy-of-controls pyramid from elimination and substitution through engineering, administrative controls, and PPE.](../figures/FIG-02-19-003-hazard-controls-and-the-control-hierarchy.png)

## 19.4 Hazard Ratings and GHS Labels

The Handbook shows a four-quadrant fire/hazard diamond and GHS label elements.

The hazard diamond communicates health, flammability, instability, and special hazards. GHS labels use product identifier, signal word, hazard statements, precautionary statements, pictograms, supplier identification, and other required information.

Do not confuse the hazard diamond's numeric ratings with GHS categories; they are different communication systems.

![FIG-02-19-004: Side-by-side hazard diamond and GHS label anatomy, with callout that their ratings/categories are separate systems.](../figures/FIG-02-19-004-hazard-ratings-and-ghs-labels.png)

## 19.5 Safety Data Sheets

The Handbook lists the standardized 16-section SDS format. High-value sections include:

- Section 2 — hazards identification,
- Section 4 — first aid,
- Section 5 — firefighting,
- Section 6 — accidental release,
- Section 7 — handling/storage,
- Section 8 — exposure controls/PPE,
- Section 10 — stability/reactivity,
- Section 11 — toxicology.

The SDS is product-specific information; it does not replace site-specific hazard assessment.

## 19.6 PPE Selection and Limitations

PPE selection should match the hazard, exposure route, concentration, task, and equipment limitations. Section 8 of the SDS is one information source.

Typical PPE categories include eye/face protection, gloves, protective clothing, hearing protection, respiratory protection, and electrical protective equipment.

A glove compatible with one chemical may fail in another. Respirator use requires an appropriate respiratory-protection program. PPE does not make an uncontrolled process inherently safe.

## 19.7 Safety Systems and Emergency Protection

The FE specifications identify basic safety equipment such as pressure-relief devices, emergency shutoffs, fire prevention/control, and PPE.

A safety system should consider prevention, detection, control, mitigation, and emergency response. Examples include relief valves, interlocks, gas detectors, barriers, alarms, ventilation, and emergency shutdown systems.

This system-level framework is guide-developed. The exact required safeguards depend on codes, process hazards, and jurisdiction.

![FIG-02-19-007: Layer-of-protection diagram showing prevention, detection, automatic control/interlock, relief/containment, alarm, PPE, and emergency response.](../figures/FIG-02-19-007-safety-systems-and-emergency-protection.png)

2. 2. Risk index=8×0.05=0.40 in that simple screening model.

3. 3. Break a task into steps, identify hazards, and document safer methods/controls.

4. 4. Elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative/work-practice controls, PPE.

5. 5. Section 8.

6. 6. Section 10.

8. 8. It depends on correct selection, fit, condition, and worker use and does not remove the source hazard.

9. 9. It prevents or changes operation when a monitored condition is unsafe or a required condition is not met.

10. 10. SDS information is product-based; actual task, quantity, equipment, ventilation, interactions, and emergency conditions are site-specific.
