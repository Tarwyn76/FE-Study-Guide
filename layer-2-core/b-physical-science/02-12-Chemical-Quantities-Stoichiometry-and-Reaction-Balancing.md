## 12.1 The Mole and Avogadro's Number

The mole connects microscopic particles to laboratory-scale quantities. The Handbook gives

\[
1\ \text{mol}=6.02\times10^{23}\ \text{particles}.
\]

The word **particle** must match the substance: atoms for monatomic species, molecules for molecular compounds, ions for an ionic species, or formula units for an ionic solid.

Moles are often the central accounting unit in reaction calculations. Convert mass to moles before using stoichiometric coefficients unless the coefficients are already expressed on a mass basis.

## 12.2 Molar Mass and Composition

Molar mass is obtained by summing periodic-table atomic weights according to the chemical formula. For water,

\[
M_{H_2O}=2M_H+M_O.
\]

The mass-mole relation is

\[
n=\frac{m}{M},
\]

where \(n\) is amount in moles, \(m\) is mass, and \(M\) is molar mass.

Mass fraction of element \(i\) in a compound is

\[
w_i=\frac{\text{mass of element }i\text{ in one mole of compound}}{\text{molar mass of compound}}.
\]

Keep units explicit because g/mol and kg/kmol are numerically equivalent but not dimensionally interchangeable unless converted consistently.

## 12.3 Balancing Chemical Reactions

A balanced chemical equation conserves each chemical element. Adjust **coefficients**, not subscripts. Changing a subscript changes the substance itself.

A reliable procedure is:

1. write correct formulas for reactants and products;
2. count atoms of each element;
3. balance elements appearing in the fewest species first;
4. leave elemental oxygen and hydrogen until later when practical;
5. reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio;
6. recheck every element and charge.

For ionic redox equations, both atoms and net charge must balance.

## 12.4 Stoichiometric Ratios

For a balanced reaction

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow cC+dD,
\]

the coefficients give molar ratios:

\[
\frac{n_A}{a}=\frac{n_B}{b}=\frac{n_C}{c}=\frac{n_D}{d}
\]

for amounts that react exactly according to stoichiometry.

A coefficient is not a mass ratio. Convert moles to mass using molar mass after applying the mole ratio.

Stoichiometry also supports gas-volume ratios when gases are at the same temperature and pressure because equal moles of ideal gas occupy equal volumes under identical conditions.

## 12.5 Limiting and Excess Reactants

When reactants are not supplied in exact stoichiometric proportion, one reactant is consumed first. This is the **limiting reactant** and it controls the maximum amount of product.

For each reactant, calculate the amount of product it could form if it were the only limiting species. The smallest predicted product amount identifies the limiting reactant.

The other reactants are in excess. Excess remaining is initial amount minus stoichiometric amount consumed.

![FIG-02-12-005: Limiting-reactant table showing available moles, stoichiometric requirement, possible product from each reactant, and identification of the limiting reactant.](../figures/FIG-02-12-005-limiting-and-excess-reactants.png)

## 12.6 Theoretical Yield, Actual Yield, and Percent Yield

The **theoretical yield** is the maximum product predicted from the limiting reactant and balanced equation. The **actual yield** is measured product.

Percent yield is

\[
\%Y=\frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}}\times100\%.
\]

Values below 100% can result from incomplete conversion, side reactions, losses in separation, or measurement error. A reported yield above 100% usually indicates wet/impure product, measurement problems, or an inconsistent basis.

## 12.7 Gas Stoichiometry and Standard Molar Volume

The Handbook gives the molar volume of an ideal gas at 0°C and 1 atm as approximately

\[
22.4\ \text{L/mol}.
\]

At those stated conditions,

\[
V=n(22.4\ \text{L/mol}).
\]

Do not use 22.4 L/mol at arbitrary temperature and pressure. Outside the stated standard condition, use the ideal-gas law or other supplied gas relation.

Gas stoichiometry is still mole stoichiometry: balance the reaction, use coefficient ratios, then convert moles to volume at the appropriate state.

## Practice Problem Solutions

1. 1. N=0.250(6.02×10^23)=1.505×10^23 molecules.

2. 2. n=36.03/18.015=2.000 mol.

3. 3. C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O.

4. 4. 2.5×5=12.5 mol O2.

5. 5. 5 mol H2 would require 2.5 mol O2, but only 2 mol are available; O2 is limiting.

6. 6. 2 mol O2 × (2 mol H2O/1 mol O2)=4 mol H2O.

7. 7. 3.4/4×100%=85%.

8. 8. V=1.50×22.4=33.6 L.

9. 9. A subscript defines the chemical identity/composition; changing it creates a different substance.

10. 10. Product may be wet/impure or measurement/basis may be inconsistent; true chemical yield above 100% requires investigation.
