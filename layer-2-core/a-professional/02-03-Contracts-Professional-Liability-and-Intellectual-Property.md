## 03.1 Engineering Agreements and Scope

An engineering agreement should define what services are being provided, by whom, on what schedule, for what compensation, and with what deliverables and assumptions. Scope clarity matters because technical obligations and business obligations interact.

Scope growth without documented change can create technical and commercial risk. Conversely, a narrow written scope does not excuse violation of public-safety duties or licensure law.

For exam-style reasoning, separate contractual allocation of work from nondelegable professional duties.

## 03.2 Standard of Care and Professional Liability

Engineering liability questions often turn on whether professional services met the applicable standard of care—the level of skill and diligence reasonably expected under the circumstances. This is not a guarantee of perfect results.

Negligence generally requires more than an unfavorable outcome. A poor result can occur even when reasonable professional care was exercised; conversely, an engineer can act negligently even if damage has not yet occurred.

This section is guide-developed legal context. The Handbook does not provide a standalone tort-law formula.

![FIG-02-03-002: Comparison of professional standard of care versus guarantee of perfection. A timeline shows reasonable investigation/design/documentation versus careless omission.](../figures/FIG-02-03-002-standard-of-care-and-professional-liability.png)

## 03.3 Risk Allocation and Contract Clauses

Contracts may allocate responsibilities for schedule, cost, changes, indemnity, insurance, intellectual property, and limitation of liability. The precise enforceability of clauses depends on jurisdiction and contract language.

Engineers should understand what risks they are accepting and avoid promises inconsistent with professional standards or insurance coverage.

For FE preparation, the key is conceptual: contract clauses can allocate commercial risk, but they cannot authorize unethical conduct or unlicensed practice.

![FIG-02-03-003: Risk-allocation matrix assigning design, construction means/methods, schedule, site information, and change responsibilities to project parties, with a warning that ethics and licensure duties remain outside ordinary risk transfer.](../figures/FIG-02-03-003-risk-allocation-and-contract-clauses.png)

## 03.4 Confidentiality, Records, and Documentation

Good project records document decisions, assumptions, calculations, communications, approvals, and changes. Documentation helps future engineers understand why a decision was made and supports accountability.

Confidential information remains subject to the professional rules discussed in Chapter 02-01. Recordkeeping should not become selective editing designed to create a misleading history.

A professional record should be contemporaneous, factual, and traceable.

## 03.5 Patents and Inventions

The Handbook defines a patent as a property right granted to an inventor by the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office. It identifies utility, design, and plant patents and notes that U.S. patent rights are territorial.

For engineers, the first practical question is often ownership: employment agreements and project contracts may affect who owns an invention or who has rights to use it.

This chapter does not determine ownership for a specific situation; it teaches the protection category and the need to check governing agreements.

## 03.6 Trademarks, Copyrights, and Trade Secrets

A trademark identifies the source of goods or services and distinguishes them from others. Copyright protects original works of authorship. A trade secret protects valuable business information that derives advantage from not being generally known and is maintained as secret.

The protection categories overlap only in limited ways; they are not interchangeable. A product name may be trademarked while software source code is copyrighted and a manufacturing process is maintained as a trade secret.

The exam task is usually classification.

![FIG-02-03-006: Four-column IP comparison: patent protects invention/design, trademark identifies source, copyright protects original expression, trade secret protects confidential business know-how.](../figures/FIG-02-03-006-trademarks-copyrights-and-trade-secrets.png)

## 03.7 Engineering Judgment at the Legal/Ethical Boundary

A contract can tell you what the parties agreed to do. Professional rules tell you what a licensed engineer may or must do. Intellectual-property law determines protection rights. Liability principles address consequences of unreasonable professional conduct.

When these systems overlap, do not collapse them into one rule. First identify the type of issue: contract, licensure/ethics, intellectual property, or liability. Then apply the correct source of authority.

For actual practice, legal counsel may be necessary. For the FE exam, the goal is to recognize the category and the controlling professional principle.

## Practice Problems

1. A contract's scope statement is ambiguous about a deliverable. What should the project team do before relying on assumptions?

2. A design performs poorly, but the engineer followed reasonable professional practice. Does the poor outcome alone prove negligence?

3. A contract attempts to transfer responsibility for an engineer's ethical duty to protect public welfare. Is that effective?

4. A project team changes a key assumption. What should a defensible project record contain?

5. An engineer develops a new machine mechanism. Which intellectual-property category most directly protects an invention?

6. A company wants to protect its product name. Which IP category is most directly relevant?

7. A software manual is copied without permission. Which IP category is most directly implicated?

8. A confidential manufacturing method gives a firm economic advantage. Which protection category may apply if secrecy is maintained?

9. A PE is asked to seal work not under their responsible charge. Is this mainly contract, licensure/ethics, IP, or liability?

10. A client disputes whether an engineer exercised reasonable professional care. Which issue category is central?

## Practice Problem Solutions

1. Clarify and document the scope, deliverables, assumptions, responsibilities, and any change before proceeding.

2. No. Negligence concerns breach of the applicable standard of care, not merely an unfavorable result.

3. No. Contract terms cannot authorize unethical or unlawful professional conduct.

4. The original assumption, date and basis of the change, analysis of impacts, communications, approvals, and resulting decision.

5. Patent.

6. Trademark.

7. Copyright.

8. Trade secret.

9. Licensure/ethics and responsible charge.

10. Professional liability/standard of care.
