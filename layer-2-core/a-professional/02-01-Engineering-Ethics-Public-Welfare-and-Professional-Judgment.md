## On the Board Today

Engineering ethics on the FE exam is not a vocabulary exercise. The Handbook places public welfare first, then gives rules governing competence, truthful communication, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, and conduct toward other licensees. The hard questions are usually scenarios: several duties are true at the same time, and you must identify which duty controls.

## 01.1 Public Welfare Comes First

The Handbook states that a licensee's first and foremost responsibility is to safeguard the health, safety, and welfare of the public. That priority matters when a client's schedule, cost target, or preferred answer conflicts with a safety concern.

The ethical sequence is not "obey the client unless something feels wrong." It is: identify the public-welfare issue, document the engineering basis, communicate it to the responsible parties, and escalate as required when professional judgment is overruled and the public is endangered.

A useful exam rule is to separate inconvenience from danger. A design change that costs money is not, by itself, an ethical violation. The question becomes ethical when pressure is used to suppress or distort a material safety, health, or welfare concern.

## 01.2 Competence and Scope of Practice

Licensees should undertake assignments only when qualified by education or experience in the specific technical fields involved. Competence is not the same as general intelligence or willingness to learn while signing the final work.

A project may contain several technical segments. A licensee may coordinate an entire project while qualified professionals take responsibility for the portions they prepared. The ethical problem occurs when a person signs or seals work in a technical area in which they lack competence or did not exercise responsible charge.

On an exam scenario, look for the phrase "qualified by education or experience." If the engineer lacks that foundation, the correct action is normally to obtain qualified assistance or decline responsibility for that technical segment.

![FIG-02-01-002: Assignment decision flowchart: qualified by education/experience? yes -> accept; no -> obtain qualified specialist or decline. A separate branch shows project coordination with technical segments signed by responsible qualified engineers.](../figures/FIG-02-01-002-competence-and-scope-of-practice.png)

### Worked Example 2 — Apply the Rule

Do not let schedule or hierarchy substitute for competence, responsible charge, truthful communication, or the applicable legal authority.

## 01.3 Truthful and Objective Communication

Professional documents, statements, and testimony should include relevant and pertinent information in an objective and truthful manner. Engineers should not omit a material fact merely because it weakens a client's preferred position.

Public professional opinions should be founded on adequate knowledge of the facts and a competent evaluation of the subject matter. When a statement is sponsored or paid for by an interested party, the relationship should be disclosed.

The practical test is whether a reasonable decision maker would be misled by what was said, omitted, exaggerated, or presented without necessary qualification.

![FIG-02-01-003: Two report excerpts side by side: one objective statement includes assumptions, uncertainty, and material limitations; the other omits a known limitation. Callouts identify truthful completeness versus misleading omission.](../figures/FIG-02-01-003-truthful-and-objective-communication.png)

### Worked Example 3 — Apply the Rule

Classify the issue first. Contract, licensure/ethics, intellectual property, and liability can overlap, but they are not the same body of rules.

## 01.4 Conflicts of Interest and Compensation

A conflict of interest exists when another interest could influence, or appear to influence, professional judgment. The Handbook requires disclosure of known or potential conflicts to employers or clients.

Compensation from more than one party for services on the same project is not automatically forbidden, but the circumstances must be fully disclosed and agreed to in writing by all interested parties. Secret compensation is the problem.

The exam often tests appearance as well as actual corruption. A disclosed relationship can still require management, but an undisclosed relationship undermines trust even if the engineer believes the judgment remained unbiased.

## 01.5 Confidentiality and Proper Use of Information

Facts, data, and information obtained in a professional capacity generally should not be disclosed without consent, except as authorized or required by law or rules. Confidentiality protects the client or employer, but it is not a license to conceal danger or violate law.

Engineers also should not use confidential information obtained through an assignment for personal profit without consent. The ethical issue is not only disclosure; misuse can occur even when information is never published.

When confidentiality and public safety appear to conflict, identify the governing law or rule and the severity of the public-welfare issue. Do not invent a blanket rule that confidentiality always wins or always loses.

## 01.6 Reporting Violations and Errors

The Handbook states that licensees who know or have reason to believe a person or firm violated applicable engineering or surveying rules or laws shall report it to the board and cooperate as requested. It also requires a reasonable effort to inform another licensee when that licensee's work appears to contain a material discrepancy, error, or omission that may affect public health, safety, or welfare, unless reporting is legally prohibited.

These duties are not invitations to make reckless accusations. The phrase "knowledge or reason to believe" calls for a factual basis.

On exam questions, distinguish a minor stylistic disagreement from a material discrepancy that could affect the public.

## 01.7 Professional Judgment in Difficult Cases

The Handbook explicitly notes that codes cannot mechanically answer every ethical problem. Conceptual issues may turn on disputed definitions; factual issues may involve incomplete information; and engineering decisions may require tradeoffs among risk, benefit, safety, and economics.

A disciplined approach is to identify stakeholders, separate facts from assumptions, identify the controlling professional duties, seek missing information, consider consequences, and document the reasoning.

The point of professional judgment is not to escape the rules. It is to apply the rules coherently when facts are incomplete or duties interact.

## Practice Problems

1. A project manager asks you to remove a material safety limitation from a report because it may delay approval. What should you do?

2. You are asked to take responsibility for a technical segment outside your demonstrated competence. What is the proper response?

3. A client relationship creates a financial interest that could appear to influence your recommendation. What action is required first?

4. You discover a material error that could affect public safety. Outline the professional response.

5. A confidential project fact is requested by a third party. What questions must be answered before disclosure?

6. A senior person directs you to sign a statement you know omits a material fact. How should you respond?

7. Two professional duties appear to conflict. Describe a structured way to resolve the conflict.

8. A colleague's work contains a material discrepancy that may affect public welfare. What should you do?

9. A contractor offers you a personal gift while you are evaluating its work. What professional concern is raised?

10. A public technical opinion is requested before the underlying facts are adequately known. What is the proper response?


---

## Practice Problem Solutions

1. Preserve objective and truthful communication; include the material safety information and escalate if public welfare is implicated.

2. Obtain qualified assistance or decline responsibility for that technical segment; do not sign or seal outside competence.

3. Disclose the known or potential conflict to the affected employer/client and follow any consent requirements.

4. Verify the factual basis, notify responsible parties, protect the public, and report or escalate as applicable.

5. Determine consent, legal authorization or requirement, and whether a controlling public-welfare duty changes the ordinary confidentiality rule.

6. Refuse to make a misleading statement; provide relevant facts objectively and document the issue.

7. Separate facts from assumptions, identify stakeholders and controlling duties, seek missing information, protect public welfare, and document the rationale.

8. Make a reasonable effort to inform the responsible licensee and follow applicable reporting rules if public welfare is at risk.

9. A gratuity/conflict-of-interest concern; do not accept improper consideration connected with professional judgment.

10. Do not issue the opinion until it is founded on adequate knowledge and competent evaluation.
