## 61.1 Municipal solid-waste generation, composition, and mass balance

Solid-waste systems track material by mass and volume through collection, recycling, composting, thermal processing, and disposal. Moisture and density affect volume and energy content.

\[\text{generated}=\text{recycled}+\text{processed}+\text{disposed}+\Delta\text{storage}\]

![FIG-03-61-001: Municipal solid-waste flow from generation through recycling, composting, WTE, and landfill.](../figures/FIG-03-61-001-municipal-solid-waste-generation-composition-and-mass-balance.png)

### Worked Example 1

**Problem.** Diverting 30% of a 100-ton/day stream leaves 70 tons/day for other management routes.

**Solution.** A 30% diversion of \(100\ {\rm ton/day}\) removes \(0.30(100)=30\ {\rm ton/day}\). The remaining stream is therefore \(100-30=\mathbf{70\ ton/day}\) for other management routes.

---

## 61.2 Collection, transfer, recycling, composting, and waste-to-energy

Solid-waste management is a system problem. High recovery can reduce disposal volume but requires collection, processing, markets, and contamination control.

\[\text{system cost/environmental impact}=f(\text{collection, transfer, processing, recovery, disposal})\]

![FIG-03-61-002: Collection routes feeding transfer, materials recovery, composting, WTE, and landfill facilities.](../figures/FIG-03-61-002-collection-transfer-recycling-composting-and-waste-to-energy.png)

### Worked Example 2

**Problem.** A transfer station can reduce long-haul transportation cost when collection vehicles would otherwise travel far to disposal.

**Solution.** A transfer station can consolidate waste into higher-capacity long-haul vehicles. When disposal is distant, this reduces the time collection vehicles spend away from their routes and can reduce transportation cost and fuel use despite the added transfer operation.

---

## 61.3 Landfill liners, leachate, gas, and cover water balance

Modern landfills manage water infiltration, leachate, gas, settlement, and cover performance. The Handbook provides simplified liner breakthrough, gas-flux, and cover-water-balance relationships.

\[\Delta S_{LC}=P-R-ET-PER_{sw}\]

![FIG-03-61-003: Engineered landfill with composite liner, leachate collection, gas wells, daily/final cover, stormwater, and groundwater monitoring.](../figures/FIG-03-61-003-landfill-liners-leachate-gas-and-cover-water-balance.png)

### Worked Example 3

**Problem.** Reducing percolation through final cover generally reduces leachate generation.

**Solution.** In a landfill-cover water balance, precipitation is partitioned among runoff, evapotranspiration, storage change, and percolation. Reducing percolation through the cover therefore directly reduces the water available to become **leachate**.

## 61.4 Hazardous-waste compatibility and characterization

Hazardous wastes must be characterized before mixing, storage, transport, or treatment. Incompatibility can cause heat, gas generation, fire, pressure, or toxic releases.

\[\text{characterize}\rightarrow\text{segregate incompatible wastes}\rightarrow\text{select treatment/storage}\]

![FIG-03-61-004: Hazardous-waste compatibility matrix and segregated storage concept.](../figures/FIG-03-61-004-hazardous-waste-compatibility-and-characterization.png)

### Worked Example 4

**Problem.** Two individually manageable wastes may be unsafe to combine if they react vigorously.

**Solution.** Compatibility must be evaluated before combining hazardous wastes. Acids/bases, oxidizers/fuels, cyanide- or sulfide-bearing wastes, and water-reactive materials can produce heat, fire, pressure, or toxic gas even when each waste can be managed safely by itself.

---

## 61.5 Hazardous and radioactive waste treatment/disposal

Treatment selection depends on hazard, chemistry, phase, volume, and final disposal requirements. Stabilization can reduce mobility without destroying contaminant mass; thermal treatment can destroy suitable organics.

\[\text{treatment}= \text{physical}+\text{chemical}+\text{biological}+\text{thermal as appropriate}\]

![FIG-03-61-005: Physical, chemical, biological, thermal, stabilization, and secure-disposal pathways for hazardous waste.](../figures/FIG-03-61-005-hazardous-and-radioactive-waste-treatment-disposal.png)

### Worked Example 5

**Problem.** Solidification/stabilization primarily reduces mobility rather than destroying metals.

**Solution.** Solidification/stabilization primarily changes the physical/chemical form to reduce leaching and mobility. Metals are elements and are generally **not destroyed** by this treatment; they remain in the treated mass.

---

## 61.6 Energy sources, efficiency, and environmental burdens

Energy technologies differ in greenhouse gases, air emissions, water use, land use, waste, reliability, and lifecycle impacts. Comparisons require a consistent functional basis.

\[\text{impact intensity}=\frac{\text{environmental burden}}{\text{energy delivered}}\]

![FIG-03-61-006: Matrix comparing conventional and alternative energy sources across carbon, air, water, land, waste, and reliability dimensions.](../figures/FIG-03-61-006-energy-sources-efficiency-and-environmental-burdens.png)

### Worked Example 6

**Problem.** Comparing emissions per plant-year can mislead if two plants deliver very different amounts of electricity.

**Solution.** Environmental comparison should use a functional denominator such as emissions per MWh delivered. Plant-year totals can be misleading if two facilities produce very different useful energy during that year.

---

## 61.7 Carbon footprint, water-energy links, and lifecycle perspective

Carbon footprint aggregates greenhouse gases on a common CO2-equivalent basis. Energy production also affects thermal discharges and water withdrawal/consumption; lifecycle boundaries must be stated.

\[\mathrm{CO_2e}=\sum m_i\,GWP_i\]

![FIG-03-61-007: Energy lifecycle from resource extraction through conversion, transmission/use, emissions, water use, waste, and end-of-life.](../figures/FIG-03-61-007-carbon-footprint-water-energy-links-and-lifecycle-perspective.png)

### Worked Example 7

**Problem.** A lower direct-emission technology can still have upstream material or construction impacts that belong in a lifecycle comparison.

**Solution.** Lifecycle assessment extends beyond direct stack or operating emissions. Materials extraction, construction, fuel production, water use, maintenance, decommissioning, and other upstream/downstream burdens can change the comparative result.

## Integrated Worked Examples

### Worked Example 8

**Problem.** A treatment calculation predicts a negative effluent concentration. What does that indicate?

**Solution.** A negative waste flow or disposal mass violates the overall material balance. Recheck diversion fractions, recycling/processing streams, storage change, and whether a residual from one process was omitted from another management route.
